AWS

arise vs grafana vs langraph

I’m assuming you mean Arize (the hackathon co-host), Grafana, and LangGraph. These aren’t really three competitors. Two are observability tools, and one is a different kind of tool entirely.

Arize (Phoenix) is observability built specifically for LLM and agent apps. It records traces of each prompt, tool call, and model response, and it has evals, where an LLM scores outputs for things like hallucination or relevance. It’s open source and works with most frameworks, including LangGraph, through OpenTelemetry-based instrumentation. Use it to understand why your agent did something and to measure whether it’s good.

Grafana is general-purpose monitoring and dashboards, mostly for infrastructure and application metrics, logs, and traces (usually paired with Prometheus, Loki, or Tempo). It’s excellent for “is my server healthy, how’s latency, what’s the error rate.” It isn’t LLM-aware out of the box, so it has no built-in evals, prompt views, or hallucination scoring, though you can build LLM dashboards with extra setup.

LangGraph isn’t observability at all. It’s a framework for building agents, where you define a graph of steps with state, loops, branching, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints. It’s what you’d write your agent in. Its own observability companion is LangSmith, which is the closest direct competitor to Arize.

What it is	Role
Arize / Phoenix	LLM observability + evals	Debug and measure your agent
Grafana	Infra/app monitoring dashboards	Monitor system health
LangGraph	Agent-building framework	Build your agent
For a hackathon, a sensible stack is to build the agent in LangGraph (or any framework), instrument it with Phoenix so you can show traces and eval results in your demo, and skip Grafana unless you’re also running real infrastructure. Since Arize is a sponsor, showing meaningful use of it, like an eval that caught and fixed a failure, would help with the bonus sponsor criterion.

Short answer: it’s possible, and the money is real. The part that isn’t proven is making an agent reliable enough to do it unsupervised.

Where money is sitting unclaimed in NYC
1. NY State unclaimed funds (the best hackathon target)

The state’s pool has passed $20 billion across more than 55 million accounts. It’s made up of old bank accounts, uncashed checks, utility deposits, and unused gift cards. 
waer
In NYC, Manhattan residents are owed almost $3 billion across 5.3 million accounts, and Brooklyn and Queens each have more than $1 billion outstanding. 
nbcnewyork
There’s no fee and no time limit to claim, and the state returns about $2 million a day. Claiming is free, so users could do it themselves; the problem is that most people never check. 
ny
Amounts of $250 or less are now released directly with no claim needed. The state’s annual report also mentions an expedited process for claims up to $5,000. Verify the current threshold before you build logic around it. 
nbcnewyork
2. Tax credits (EITC and NYC credits)

An estimated one-fifth of eligible households nationally fail to claim the federal EITC. 
sufe
NYC, the state, and the IRS are currently trying to reach city residents who appear eligible but didn’t claim the EITC on their 2022 returns, and NYC sent second notices in March 2026. That’s direct evidence that money is going unclaimed right now. 
irs
An older city estimate put it at 150,000 residents who never claim, worth more than $160 million. That document is undated and likely old, so treat it as a rough magnitude. 
nyc
Free help already exists. The city’s Free Tax Prep program covers the EITC and the NYC child care credit. So a paid agent would need to be better than free, which is a competitive risk. 
secretnyc
3. SNAP and other benefits

A Columbia study found about 1 in 4 eligible New Yorkers doesn’t use SNAP, which adds up to more than 700,000 people. That’s a 2023 report, so the numbers may have moved. 
robinhood
Seniors and immigrant households are described as disproportionately under-enrolled. 
citylimits
Can an agent actually do the paperwork?
Evidence that it’s possible: Propel tested Claude Cowork and ChatGPT Agent on SNAP applications. Both were willing to help. Also, AI agents are already being used to fill out forms and submit benefit applications, and early research suggests most people using them are genuinely eligible. 
propel
renascence

Evidence it isn’t reliable yet: In that same Propel test, both agents failed to enter all the provided application data accurately. It was an internal test with no real applications submitted. That finding matters for your project: accuracy is the main weakness, which makes Arize evals the proof you need rather than a bonus feature. 
propel

Others are moving in: Code for America is partnering with Anthropic on Claude-based tools for benefit caseworkers. Nava, with Benefits Data Trust, is exploring an agent that completes applications with caseworker supervision at key decision points. That validates the idea, but it also means you’re not alone, and the serious players keep a human in the loop. Propel has started drafting a standard covering user consent, safe interfaces, and auditable interactions. That’s a good checklist for your design. 
Our Anthropic partnership +2

What I couldn’t verify
Whether portals like ACCESS HRA, IRS, or insurer sites allow automated browser access under their terms, and how they handle MFA and CAPTCHAs. Check this before claiming it works on real portals.
Whether NY restricts fees on finding unclaimed money for someone, or on charging for benefits help. A percentage-of-recovery model needs a legal check.
Current eligibility rules for each program. Those change, so pull them from official sources.
